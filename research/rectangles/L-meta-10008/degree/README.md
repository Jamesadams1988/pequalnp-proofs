# How many rows can use only two rectangles?

For the assigned 31-by-31 matrix, covering all zero cells with at most two rectangle memberships per row requires **exactly 24 rectangles**. In any unrestricted 17-rectangle cover, at least **nine rows** must belong to three or more rectangles. The same holds for at least nine columns.

More precisely, select some rows and cover all their zeros against all 31 columns, with at most two rectangles per selected row. If the rectangle budget is m, the **largest possible number of selected rows** is m for m≤3, and min(31, m+⌊m/4⌋+1) for 4≤m≤24. At m=17 this is 22. Attainment concerns the selected-row submatrix. Whether an unrestricted cover of the entire matrix exists with 17 rectangles is open here. The script checks all budgets and both orientations. [Construction](profile.py) · [Matrix](rows.json)

## Counting proof

Interpret rows as points and columns as lines. Each line contains six points, any pair of points has a unique joining line, and two lines meet in one point. All these incidences follow from the matrix. Restrict the row sides of the rectangles to the selected points. A rectangle with a singleton row side is called private here. Charge each point with a private rectangle to one distinct such rectangle. A point belonging to only one rectangle must have a private one. Otherwise the rectangle would also contain some q different from p, and no line through q avoiding p could cover its zero in row p.

Every remaining point p therefore occurs in exactly two nonsingleton row sets A and B. They intersect only at p. If a second point q lay in both, neither rectangle could cover any line through q avoiding p. For every x in A other than p and y in B other than p, the line xy must go through p. Otherwise it meets both row sets and cannot be covered at p. Fixing x and then y proves A and B both lie on the same line. Since each contains at least two points, its carrier line is unique. [Lean support lemmas](../review/TwoSupports.lean)

Group those remaining points and their supports by carrier line. Different groups use disjoint sets of rectangle indices. In one group write e for the number of points and v for the number of distinct rectangle indices. Each point has a distinct unordered pair of indices. Two points with the same pair would violate the intersection property. Thus e≤min(6, binom(v,2)). A positive saving e−v requires v≥4, and is at most one unless e=6. Only one group can have six points, since two distinct full lines intersect and the groups partition the remaining points. Consequently at most ⌊m/4⌋ groups save anything, and at most one of those can save an extra point. Private points account for at most one point per private rectangle, giving the asserted upper bound. For m≤3 no group can save a point. The Lean file verifies the capacity and aggregate inequality from these component hypotheses. The preceding paragraph supplies the grouping argument. [Arithmetic proof](ComponentBound.lean)

For the construction, take all six lines through one point. Use one entire line, then the five-point sections made by deleting the common point from the other five lines. In each funded section, label its points by distinct two-element subsets of four coordinates. Each coordinate is one rectangle: its row set is the points carrying it, and its column set is every line missing those points. Any line avoiding p intersects the section's carrier in at most one other point. Some coordinate of p excludes that other point. Four rectangles cover a whole section, and leftover budget buys individual rows. The script verifies the exact masks against every cell. Run `python3 -B profile.py` in this directory. With the pinned project built, run `lake env lean /path/to/ComponentBound.lean` from its `lean` directory.
